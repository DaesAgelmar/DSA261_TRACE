Week 1 — How Do You Know?

Information seeking, source tracing, verification, and epistemic agency

TipThis week’s central question

How do you know that something you see online is true?

This week is not simply about “finding the correct news story.” The deeper goal is to make visible why we trust a claim, what evidence changes our judgment, and how we trace information back to inspectable evidence.

TRACE this week

Predict → Encounter → Probe → Explain → Decide → Verify

TRACE is not an answer formula. It is a working protocol for keeping epistemic control with you when you encounter a claim, search result, algorithmic decision, or LLM output.

Learning goals

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  1. explain the difference between a claim, a source, and evidence;
  2. distinguish the page where information first reaches you from the original source of that information;
  3. design different search queries for the same information need;
  4. evaluate a source by going beyond the page itself rather than relying only on its appearance;
  5. test a claim against at least one independent source;
  6. explain why your confidence changed—or did not change—after verification.

TRACE Lab

Session 1 — From impression to investigation

1. Today’s Case — The claim arrives first

At the beginning of class, you will be shown a claim taken from a real digital environment.

Do not search yet.

First, record the following individually:

  • Probability that the claim is correct: 0–100
  • Initial judgment: Trust / Doubt / Unsure
  • The single strongest signal behind that judgment
WarningTRACE rule

Record your prediction before seeing the evidence. Saying “I already thought that” after the result is known is not evidence of learning.

2. Predict

Complete this sentence in one minute:

“At this point, I am ___% confident in this claim because …”

Then compare only your reasoning with the person next to you. Do not yet argue about who is correct.

3. Encounter

Now inspect the page, post, or artifact containing the claim in full.

Record:

  • Who is making the claim?
  • Where was it published?
  • Is there a date?
  • Is a source cited?
  • If there is a link, does it lead to the original source or merely to another report?
  • Is the claim specific enough to be tested?

4. Probe I — Search differently

Investigate the same claim using at least three meaningfully different search strategies.

Query A — Exact phrase
Search a distinctive part of the claim in quotation marks.

Query B — Source-oriented
Search for the institution, researcher, report, dataset, or document supposedly behind the claim.

Query C — Evidence-oriented
Search for the data, report, study, primary document, or official record that could actually test the claim—not merely another page repeating it.

Do not stop at the first search result.

5. Skill Studio — Source tracing

Try to reconstruct this chain:

Claim → Publisher → Cited source → Original source → Independent evidence

If a link in the chain cannot be found, record that as a result. “I could not find it” can be an important finding when the missing link should exist.

6. Probe II — Leave the page

Do not evaluate an unfamiliar source only through its own self-description.

Open new tabs and investigate:

  • What do independent sources say about the publisher or author?
  • How is the same claim reported elsewhere?
  • Is there an earlier or more original version?
  • Are apparently different reports actually independent, or are they all repeating the same source?

7. Explain — What changed?

Short class discussion:

  • What signal influenced your initial trust judgment most strongly?
  • Which query produced the most useful result?
  • Did many repeated reports actually represent many independent pieces of evidence?
  • Did the claim change when you reached the primary or original source?

Short break

Session 2 — From investigation to defensible judgment

8. Pair Challenge — Trace before you trust

Work in pairs on a second claim.

Each pair must produce four outputs:

  1. One-sentence claim — What exactly is being claimed?
  2. Best query — Which search query was most productive?
  3. Strongest evidence — What is the strongest evidence you found?
  4. Rejected result — Give one result you deliberately did not use and explain why.

When groups compare results, compare not only the verdicts but also the search paths that produced them.

9. Decide — Make a defensible judgment

Classify the claim using one of the following categories:

  • Supported — Available evidence supports the claim.
  • Contradicted — Credible evidence conflicts with the claim.
  • Unsupported — There is not enough evidence to support the claim.
  • Missing context — Part of the claim may be correct, but missing context materially changes its meaning.
  • Unresolved — The available public evidence does not support a reliable conclusion.

Defend your judgment in two sentences.

10. Verify — Independent evidence

Before closing the case, ask:

What evidence independent of the original claimant supports my final judgment?

If you cannot find independent evidence, recalibrate your confidence accordingly.

11. Recalibrate

Return to your initial confidence score.

  • Initial confidence: ___ / 100
  • Final confidence: ___ / 100
  • Change: ___

The key question is not simply “Was my first guess correct?”

Did my judgment change as much as the evidence warranted?

After class

Task 01 — Source Trace

This is your individual TRACE evidence task for Week 1.

Choose one testable digital claim that you personally encountered during the last seven days. Your job is not merely to label the claim “true” or “false.” You must show, in a reconstructable way, how you moved from first impression to a defensible judgment.

Minimum evidence

Your submission must include:

  1. the original claim and URL or screenshot;
  2. a one-sentence statement of the claim in your own words;
  3. your 0–100 initial confidence before research;
  4. three meaningfully different search queries;
  5. the earliest/original source you could identify, or an explanation of why it could not be identified;
  6. at least one independent verification source;
  7. at least one search result you rejected and why;
  8. a Supported / Contradicted / Unsupported / Missing context / Unresolved verdict;
  9. your 0–100 final confidence;
  10. the most important evidence that changed or strengthened your judgment;
  11. a brief AI-use disclosure, if applicable.
ImportantAI rule for Task 01

You may use AI, for example to brainstorm alternative search queries or to help plan your investigation.

However, LLM output is not evidence. Your final verdict must rest on inspectable external evidence. If you use AI, disclose what you used it for and what parts of verification and judgment you performed yourself.

Submit Task 01 — Source Trace

Completion standard

Complete — A reconstructable search trail, independent evidence, and a defensible verdict are present.
Revise — A conclusion is present, but the evidence trail is incomplete or the supposed verification is not independent.
Missing — The submission contains only opinion/LLM output or does not show a verifiable process.
